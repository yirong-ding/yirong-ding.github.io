Teaching Philosophy Statement

I teach so that students can connect technical knowledge with real problems that matter to their communities. Whatever the subject, from construction economics to data analysis, my teaching centers on inclusive learning, active engagement, critical attention to equity, and the development of independent judgment. I want students not only to master the tools of their field, but also to understand the assumptions behind them and use them responsibly.

Inclusive by design

Following Universal Design for Learning, I offer several ways to engage with each idea, scaffolds for students who need them, and extensions for those ready to go further, so every student is both supported and challenged.

Learning by doing

Students apply concepts to real data and open-ended problems soon after they meet them, and see how their technical choices play out in practice.

Judgment, not just answers

I ask students to justify their reasoning, state their assumptions, and consider who is affected by a technical decision, including its equity and ethical implications.

Feedback that builds independence

I find the step where a misunderstanding begins and explain it with a concrete example. Staged work, transparent criteria, and reflection let students use feedback and learn how they learn.

Ultimately, I teach to empower students to use data, technology, and critical inquiry to build safer, more equitable, and more resilient communities.

Proposed Course

AI-Driven Disaster Resilience & Urban Informatics

Graduate course · 3 credits · In person (hybrid optional)

AI & Machine Learning Geospatial Analysis Disaster Resilience Urban Informatics Equity

Course Description

This course explores how AI, geospatial analysis, and data-driven modeling can support disaster resilience, infrastructure planning, and equitable decision-making. Students engage with real hazard data, build computational models, and apply network analysis, machine learning, and knowledge graph concepts to urban systems. Through hands-on projects, discussions, and scenario-based analysis, students gain both technical skill and a systems-level understanding of resilience.

The course is designed for graduate students in civil engineering, urban planning, computer science, geography, and related fields who have basic Python programming skills. It is ideal for those interested in disaster science, urban informatics, AI/ML applications in the built environment, or interdisciplinary research at the intersection of technology and social equity.

Prerequisites

  • Basic programming experience (Python preferred)
  • Familiarity with data analysis or GIS (helpful but not required)
  • Interest in disaster resilience, urban systems, or data science

Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  • Analyze spatial and network-based disaster impacts
  • Model accessibility, vulnerability, and resilience using Python and GIS tools
  • Evaluate equity implications of infrastructure disruptions
  • Apply AI/ML and knowledge graph methods to urban resilience problems
  • Communicate findings through visual, written, and computational artifacts

Mapping Community Isolation During Disasters

One 75-minute class session and a take-home assignment

This assignment enables students to analyze how road closures during disasters affect community accessibility and equity. Using Python, OSMnx, and GIS datasets, students will identify isolated communities and interpret the social equity implications of their findings.

In-Class Lesson Plan (75 minutes)

Timing Breakdown

0–5 min: Warm-up Discussion

Open with a question: "How might a road closure during a disaster affect different communities differently?" Collect responses on board/chat.

5–15 min: Mini-Lecture

Brief overview of network accessibility concepts, travel-time calculations, and equity considerations. Show a real-world example (e.g., Hurricane Helene road closures).

15–20 min: Setup & Instructions

Distribute notebooks and datasets. Walk through the workflow step-by-step. Answer clarifying questions.

20–50 min: Hands-on Activity

Students work individually or in pairs to complete the accessibility analysis. Instructor circulates to provide support and troubleshoot issues.

50–65 min: Small-Group Discussion

Students share findings in groups of 3-4. Discuss: Which areas were most affected? What populations might be at greatest risk? What are the limitations of this analysis?

65–75 min: Reflection & Exit Slip

Whole-class debrief. Students write a 3-minute exit slip responding to: "What surprised you about this analysis? What would you want to explore further?"

Facilitation Plan

Before class: Prepare and test notebook & datasets. Ensure all software dependencies are met.

During class: Demonstrate workflow using live coding. Circulate during hands-on time. Lead focused discussions that connect technical work to equity themes.

After class: Review exit slips for misconceptions. Post solutions and additional resources for extended learning.

Assessment

Formative assessment through:

  • Observation during hands-on work
  • Quality of small-group discussion contributions

Machine Learning for Infrastructure Disruption Prediction

Individual project with a 3–4 page technical report

This assignment challenges students to build and evaluate machine learning models that predict infrastructure disruptions during disasters. Using historical disaster data with features such as hazard intensity, infrastructure characteristics, and socioeconomic factors, students will develop regression or classification models to forecast disruption likelihood or duration. This assignment develops practical ML skills, model evaluation competency, and critical thinking about predictive modeling in disaster contexts.

Full ML workflowModel evaluationLimitations and ethicsscikit-learn

Learning Outcomes

Students will be able to:

  • Prepare and clean disaster infrastructure datasets for machine learning
  • Engineer meaningful features from spatial, temporal, and infrastructure attributes
  • Train and evaluate multiple regression/classification models using scikit-learn
  • Apply proper train-test splitting and cross-validation techniques
  • Interpret model performance and identify key predictors of disruption
  • Critically assess model limitations and ethical implications
Show the full assignment

Project Phases

Phase 1: Data Exploration & Preparation

Load the provided disaster infrastructure dataset (e.g., power outages during hurricanes, road closures during floods). Conduct exploratory data analysis (EDA): identify missing values, examine distributions, visualize correlations, and document data quality issues.

Phase 2: Feature Engineering

Create predictive features from raw data. Examples include: hazard intensity metrics (wind speed, flood depth), infrastructure age, proximity to hazard zone, socioeconomic vulnerability indices, network centrality measures, temporal features (season, time since last event). Justify your feature choices.

Phase 3: Train-Test Split & Model Training

Split dataset into training (70-80%) and test (20-30%) sets using appropriate stratification. Train at least 3 different models (e.g., Linear Regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting for regression tasks; Logistic Regression, Random Forest, XGBoost for classification). Use cross-validation on training data for hyperparameter tuning.

Phase 4: Model Evaluation & Interpretation

Evaluate models on the test set using appropriate metrics:

  • Regression: RMSE, MAE, R²
  • Classification: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC

Compare model performance. Interpret feature importance and identify key predictors of disruption.

Phase 5: Critical Analysis & Reporting

Write a 3-4 page report addressing:

  • Model performance and comparison
  • Key predictors and their practical significance
  • Limitations (data bias, missing features, generalizability)
  • Ethical considerations (fairness, equity implications)
  • Recommendations for model deployment

  • Trained model files (pickled .pkl or .joblib)

Dataset Information

Provided Dataset: Historical infrastructure disruption records from 3-5 major disaster events (hurricanes, floods, winter storms). Dataset includes:

  • Target Variable: Disruption duration (hours) OR binary disruption status
  • Features: Hazard intensity, infrastructure type/age, location (lat/lon), population served, vulnerability indices, network metrics, temporal features
  • Size: ~5,000-10,000 records

Students are encouraged to create additional features through spatial joins or external data integration.
